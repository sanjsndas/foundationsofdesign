Foundations of Interior Architecture: A Comprehensive Guide to Space, Light, and Material

By Elena Marchetti · March 2025 · 22 min read

Interior architecture sits at the intersection of building and inhabitation. It is the discipline of shaping the spaces we live in — not merely decorating them, but orchestrating volume, light, proportion, and material to create environments that support human life. This guide explores the foundational principles that underpin great interior design, from the first sketch of a floor plan to the final layer of texture.

1. The Grammar of Space: Proportion and Scale

Every room has a skeleton. Before colour, before furniture, before lighting, there is the geometry of the space itself. Proportion is the relationship between dimensions — the height of a ceiling relative to the width of a room, the length of a corridor compared to its breadth. When these ratios feel harmonious, the body relaxes. When they feel off, no amount of decoration can fully compensate.

Classical architecture relied on systems like the golden ratio, but contemporary interiors often work with more intuitive proportional systems. A simple exercise: stand in the centre of a room and observe how your eye travels. Does it land on a comfortable focal point, or does it bounce around restlessly? If the latter, the proportions may need adjustment — through architectural intervention (moving a wall, changing ceiling height) or through visual tricks (rug placement, colour blocking, strategic lighting).

2. Light as a Building Material

Light is the most underrated material in interior architecture. It is intangible, free, and transformative. The orientation of windows, the depth of reveals, the choice of glazing — these decisions shape how a space feels throughout the day and across seasons.

Natural light should be treated as a primary design element, not an afterthought. Consider the path of the sun: morning light from the east is cool and crisp; afternoon light from the west is warm and intense. South-facing rooms (in the northern hemisphere) receive consistent light throughout the day and benefit from shading devices in summer. North-facing rooms are cooler and more diffuse — ideal for studios and kitchens but potentially gloomy in living areas.

Artificial lighting should complement, not compete with, natural light. The layered approach — ambient, task, and accent — allows for flexibility. Dimmers are essential. And the colour temperature of bulbs matters: 2700K for warm, intimate spaces; 3000K for general living areas; 4000K for workspaces.

“Light is not just about seeing — it's about feeling. A room lit only from the ceiling feels institutional; a room with pools of warm light feels like home.”

3. Material Honesty and the Poetry of Surfaces

The materials we choose have a profound impact on how a space is experienced. Natural materials — wood, stone, clay, linen, wool — offer tactile richness and age gracefully. Synthetic materials can be practical and affordable, but they often lack the depth and character that make interiors feel alive.

Material honesty is the principle of letting materials be what they are: not disguising concrete as marble or vinyl as wood. This doesn't mean everything must be raw and unfinished; it means that the character of the material should be respected and celebrated. A oiled oak floor, a lime-plastered wall, a brushed brass tap — these surfaces reveal their making and their age.

4. Circulation and the Unseen Architecture

We often focus on rooms, but the spaces between rooms — corridors, thresholds, staircases — are equally important. Circulation is the circulatory system of a home. If it's cramped or confusing, the whole house feels tense. If it's generous and intuitive, the home feels calm and welcoming.

Good circulation minimises dead ends, avoids passing through one room to reach another, and provides clear visual cues about where to go. Width matters: a corridor narrower than 90cm feels tight; 110–120cm feels comfortable. Stairs should have consistent riser heights and generous treads. And thresholds — the moments between inside and outside, or between one room and another — deserve special attention. A change in floor level, a doorway with depth, a change in ceiling height: these small moves create a sense of arrival and transition.

5. The Psychology of Colour

Colour is emotional. It affects mood, perception of temperature, and even our sense of time. While colour trends come and go, certain principles remain consistent. Warm colours (reds, oranges, yellows) advance and energise; cool colours (blues, greens, greys) recede and calm. Saturation matters: muted, earthy tones tend to feel more sophisticated and timeless than high-chroma hues.

A useful approach is to build a palette around a neutral base (white, off-white, greige, or a soft earth tone), add one or two supporting colours, and use an accent colour sparingly. The 60-30-10 rule is a reliable starting point: 60% dominant neutral, 30% secondary colour, 10% accent.

But rules are made to be broken. Some of the most memorable interiors are monochromatic — layers of the same colour in different textures and tones. Others are bold and eclectic. The key is intentionality: every colour choice should support the overall concept.

6. The Details That Matter

Finally, the details. The position of a light switch. The depth of a window reveal. The way a door handle feels in the hand. The alignment of tiles. These micro-decisions accumulate into an experience of quality — or its absence.

In interior architecture, the 1% details are often what separate a good space from a great one. Consider the shadow gap where wall meets floor; the flush skirting; the perfectly centred pendant above a table. These are not extravagances; they are the marks of thoughtful design.

This guide is just the beginning. Each of these principles could fill a book. But the essential message is this: interior architecture is a holistic practice. It asks us to think about space, light, material, and human experience as an integrated whole. When we get it right, the result is not just a beautiful room — it's a better way to live.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between interior design and interior architecture? +
Interior architecture focuses on the structural and spatial aspects of a building's interior — moving walls, altering ceiling heights, designing staircases, and planning circulation. Interior design is more concerned with the finishing layers: furniture, colour, textiles, lighting, and decoration. In practice, the two disciplines overlap significantly.
How do I choose the right lighting for a living room? +
Start with ambient light (a central fixture or recessed lights on a dimmer), then add task lighting (a floor lamp beside a reading chair) and accent lighting (wall sconces, picture lights, or a small table lamp). Use warm bulbs (2700K) and avoid relying on a single bright ceiling light.
What are the best materials for a sustainable interior? +
Look for natural, renewable, and locally sourced materials: FSC-certified wood, natural stone, clay plaster, lime wash, wool, linen, and recycled metals. Avoid materials with high embodied energy or toxic finishes. Durability is also key — a material that lasts 50 years is more sustainable than one that needs replacing every 5.
How can I make a small room feel larger? +
Maximise natural light, use a consistent floor material throughout, choose furniture with legs (to show more floor), hang curtains high and wide, use mirrors strategically, and keep the palette light and tonal. Avoid clutter and heavy visual weight.
What is the 60-30-10 rule in interior design? +
It's a guideline for colour proportion: 60% of a room's colour should be a dominant neutral (walls, large furniture), 30% a secondary colour (upholstery, rugs, curtains), and 10% an accent colour (cushions, art, accessories). It helps create balanced, harmonious spaces.
